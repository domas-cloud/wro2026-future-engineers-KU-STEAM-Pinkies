# Our Testing Story

## Why Testing Was So Important for Us

Our final robot was not created in one finished version. Both the mechanics and the software improved because we repeatedly tested the robot, observed what failed, and then changed the weak parts.

We quickly learned that many things that looked fine in theory did not work equally well on the real field.

Because of that, testing became one of the most important parts of our engineering process.

## How We Judged Whether a Version Was Better

When we compared versions, we did not only ask whether the robot moved. We asked whether it moved **better**.

The most important practical criteria for us were:

- how much space the robot needed to complete a **90-degree turn**,
- and how much it drifted over a **3-meter straight drive**.

These two criteria were very useful because they measured exactly what mattered to us most:

- turning quality,
- straight-driving stability,
- and repeatability.

## Motor Testing

We tested three **N20 motors**:

- **300 rpm**,
- **600 rpm**,
- **1000 rpm**.

The 300 rpm motor was too slow for our target performance.

The 1000 rpm motor gave us the opposite problem: it did not provide enough torque.

The **600 rpm motor** gave us the best overall balance, so we kept it as our final motor.

## Steering Testing

The steering system went through three main versions.

### V1
In the first version, the wheel support created a large lever arm. Because of that, the servo had to work much harder.

### V2
In the second version, we removed that large force arm and changed the geometry so that the wheels rotated more directly in place.

This was the biggest improvement in our steering development. The servo could turn the system much more easily.

### V3
In the third version, we improved the V2 geometry further with:

- bearings mounted in the frame,
- silicone front wheels.

That gave us lower friction, better grip, and a more repeatable steering result.

## Differential Testing

From our previous experience, we already knew that a differential was necessary. But during this season we also saw that the type of differential mattered.

After changing from a metal differential to a **LEGO differential**, the robot became:

- more precise,
- less likely to jam,
- and smoother in turning.

## Front Wheel Testing

Earlier front-wheel solutions could slip. That reduced the real effect of the steering command.

After changing to **silicone front wheels**, the wheels no longer slipped, and the robot could make stronger useful turns.

This was one of the improvements that clearly helped our robot behave better on the track.

## Straight Driving Testing

At different stages, our robot could drift slightly to either side.

This was an important observation, because it showed us that straight driving was not controlled by only one part. It depended on several things together:

- steering geometry,
- wheel mounting,
- front-wheel grip,
- and differential quality.

The biggest practical improvements for straight driving came from:

- better wheel mounting,
- and changing to the LEGO differential.

After the final improvements, the robot still drifted only minimally.

## Approximate Testing Effort

We did approximately **10 test runs** while comparing the main mechanical versions.

Even though this was not a laboratory measurement process, it was enough to show very clear differences between the versions.

The most important result was simple:

**the change from steering V1 to V2 gave the biggest improvement.**

## What We Learned from Testing

The biggest lesson from testing was that the robot has to be judged as a complete system.

For example, straight driving improved not because of one single magic fix, but because several changes worked together:

- better steering geometry,
- better front-wheel grip,
- better wheel mounting,
- and better differential behaviour.

Testing helped us understand these interactions much better.

## Final Conclusion

Testing was one of the most important parts of our project.

It helped us:

- reject weaker ideas,
- confirm stronger ones,
- compare versions fairly,
- and choose our final design based on practical performance instead of assumptions.

Because of that, our final robot is not only designed — it is also **tested and iterated**.
